How to call wind on a Southeast Alaska range

On Tongass, the wind rarely reads like a prairie range. It swirls off the timber, dies in the cut, and comes back from a new direction. Learn to watch the range — not just the flag at the bench.

2026-08-02 · 7 min read · Ketchikan Rod & Gun Club

Read more than one flag

A single flag at the firing line lies often. Look mid-range and near the berm. If the near flag is calm and the far flag is kicking, hold for the far flag — that is where your bullet is spending more time.

What Southeast wind does

  • Timber edges bend and lift air. Shots past a treeline often need more hold than the bench feels.
  • Rain lanes show drift. Watch how curtains of rain lean.
  • Thermals on clear days can switch after lunch when the berm warms.

A simple calling method

  1. Pick a value for full-value wind at your distance (start from your come-up card or a known load).
  2. Estimate speed from flags: gentle move ≈ 3–5 mph, steady flap ≈ 8–10, cracking ≈ 15+.
  3. Cut the value for angle — wind from 1 or 5 o’clock is not full value.
  4. Hold or dial once. Do not “fix” mid-string unless conditions clearly change.

Practice offline. Use the wind-call trainer between range trips so the first match of the month is not your first wind lesson.

Rimfire vs centerfire

.22 LR lives in the wind. A hold that looks theatrical on a .308 is normal for rimfire steel at 100 yards. When in doubt, watch the shooter next to you — and the splash — then adjust one correction at a time.

Bring a written card. Build one with the come-up card tool before you leave town; cell service drops before the gate.
